# Variable profits are not enough

*A response to Miftah Ismail, "We should examine how close to Quranic edicts is Islamic banking" (Dawn, 6 June 2026)*

By M. Azim Abdul Majeed

Mr Miftah Ismail has written what he says bankers and economists know but rarely say aloud: that the difference between Islamic and conventional banking in Pakistan has become "more in nomenclature and less in substance." From a former finance minister the candour is rare and welcome. It is also correct, and more correct than his measured tone suggests.

He opens with the verse itself: "God has made buying and selling lawful and usury unlawful" (Al-Baqarah 2:275). It does not permit "profit" and forbid "interest." It permits trade, in which a real thing changes hands and real risk is borne, and forbids riba, an increase on money secured by the mere passage of time. That line was drawn at the level of substance, not vocabulary. A system that keeps the substance of riba and changes only its name has not crossed between the two. It has learned to spell it differently.

Take his cotton example. The bank "buys" cotton for ten million rupees and "sells" it to the company for eleven million payable in a year, while, in his words, it "doesn't actually buy the cotton or sell it," and "there is, however, paperwork to pretend this has taken place." His description of the cost-plus sale is exact. When the commodity is never genuinely acquired, held at risk, or delivered, the sale exists on paper alone. Strip the paperwork away and what remains is money advanced and a larger sum returned by prior agreement. That residue is not trade.

He then makes the observation that should settle the matter: the bank's return, whether styled Murabaha or Musharakah, tracks the State Bank's policy rate, rising when it rises, and, aside from default, no Islamic bank loses when its borrower loses. A return benchmarked to interest, owed whether the venture succeeds or fails, is interest in all but name. A financier who cannot lose when his partner loses is not a partner. He is a lender with a longer contract.

The classical jurists had a word for this: a hilah, a stratagem engineered to reach a forbidden outcome while meeting the formal conditions of a permitted one. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah exposed precisely this, the dressing of riba in the costume of sale, and Ibn Taymiyyah held that one who takes riba through a stratagem sins more gravely than one who takes it openly. The industry has not found a way around riba; it has rediscovered the hilah and scaled it. Genuine profit-and-loss partnership, the instrument that gives Islamic finance its entire moral claim, is a small fraction of what the banks actually do. The exception has become the rule, and the rule has become the brochure.

His closing analogy can be pressed harder than he presses it. Bankers defend their work by pointing to beef, lawful or unlawful by how the animal is slaughtered; he answers, rightly, that interest is more like wine, unlawful in a teacup or a wineglass alike. But their own comparison defeats them. With beef the decisive act is performed on the substance; the slaughter changes the thing itself. The bankers changed nothing in the substance. They changed the vessel, pouring the same guaranteed, risk-free return from the cup marked "interest" into the cup marked "profit," and ask us to believe the wine is lawful because the glass is now etched in Arabic.

There is a sharper detail still. Islamic banks objected to paying depositors a fixed minimum return as un-Islamic, yet cheerfully charge borrowers a fixed return tied to the policy rate. So the depositor earns less than at a conventional bank while the bank earns more, and the faithful, in Mr Ismail's words, pay for the privilege of being good Muslims. Set that beside the Quran's warning against those who keep two measures, demanding full when they receive and giving short when they give.

Why has the industry retreated so completely into disguised debt? The answer is not technical but dispositional. Genuine partnership requires the financier to accept an outcome he cannot guarantee and to lose when the venture loses. Cost-plus debt requires none of this, offering Shariah-shaped paperwork with commercial certainty, and the industry chose certainty. But trade was permitted, and riba forbidden, precisely because trade carries risk and riba removes it. To demand a guaranteed return before lifting a finger is to keep the form of Islamic finance and discard its soul.

So when Mr Ismail prescribes "variable profits and risk sharing," he is right, and few will say as much. Yet the prescription is necessary but not sufficient, for it treats the contracts and leaves untouched the money in which they are written. Suppose every Murabaha became a true Musharakah tomorrow. All of it would still be denominated in a currency created as interest-bearing debt, money conjured at the moment of lending and carrying interest from the first day. We are filtering the water with great care while the well itself is the source of the contamination. The Quran's concern was never a particular word but a reality: wealth growing without production and concentrating without limit, "so that it does not become a thing circulating only among the rich among you" (Al-Hashr 59:7).

The way out is to stop asking whether a product can be fitted to an inherited template and to ask instead whether its deep structure conforms to what the Quran and Sunnah teach about wealth, risk and justice. That means one test, applied without flinching: strip away the asset, the commodity, the trade steps, and ask what remains. If what remains is money in and more money out by prior agreement, it is riba, whatever the paperwork says. It means the courage to deploy real partnership capital and accept real loss, to fund the hospital, the farm and the school rather than only the already secure, and to attend, at last, to the honesty of the money itself.

Next year marks the silver jubilee of Pakistan's modern Islamic banking, a fitting moment to ask the question Mr Ismail's essay circles but does not quite land on. For twenty-five years we have laboured to ensure our profit is not called interest. We have been less willing to ask whether, in the sight of the One who forbade riba not as a word but as an injustice, our profit has become anything other than interest, and whether the money we count it in was ever honest to begin with.
